Why does a redundant label help?

The trajectory-mechanism study: SF3D trajectories are derived from the articulation labels — zero new bits of information — yet supervising them massively improves articulation. This is the record of how we found out why.

SegAffordance · experiments of 2026-08-24 → 25 · full numbers in experiments/INDEX.md and per-experiment notes.md · spec: docs/superpowers/specs/2026-08-25-trajectory-mechanism-design.md

1The puzzle, made precise

Our dataset writer constructs every GT trajectory from the other labels: a prismatic sample gets a straight 0.7 m ray along the axis; a revolute sample gets a 90° right-hand arc about the axis, centred at the element's height, radius = element-to-axis distance. Given axis, origin, type and the element point, the curve is a deterministic function — it can carry no information the other labels don't already carry.

The measured effect that demanded an explanation, from the supervision-ablation pair arm B (no trajectory head or loss) vs arm D (with them) — identical recipes, no consistency losses on either side, so nothing couples the heads except shared features.

Two candidate mechanisms, not mutually exclusive — the question was the split:

H1 — loss geometry. The curve is a reparameterization of the same quantities: dense (60 numbers vs a few scalars), sign-carrying (a flipped axis sweeps the arc the wrong way), and conjunction-coupled (axis ∧ origin ∧ point must be jointly right to place the curve). Same information, better-conditioned optimization problem.

H2 — feature shaping. The trajectory head's dense regression enriches the shared visual trunk; the articulation heads then read better features. (Circumstantially supported: the trajectory-only ablation arm had the best masks of all arms.)

2Toy probes first — and they killed my favorite theory

Before spending GPU money, we built a CPU toy: an MLP predicts an axis from random features; loss A is the model's angular loss (1−cos), loss B is point-MSE on an arc decoded from the prediction. Identical labels, identical information. My leading story was that the angular loss has a saddle at the antipode (a confidently-flipped axis gets no gradient) while point-space escapes it. Measured:

Flipping the axis reverses the arc — and the MSE between opposite arcs is also stationary at the exact antipode. The two profiles have the same shape; point-space is only ~1.7× larger. No saddle escape exists. Worse for the theory: trained head-to-head on a single latent, the angular loss wins — it recovers a 100%-flipped student to 0% flips while point-space gets stuck at 5.4% (real local minima), and converges tighter from scratch (0.6° vs 3.3° median).
Dead: the "sign enters linearly / saddle escape" story. Whatever helps at scale, it is not that the trajectory loss has a magically better sign landscape per-sample.

We then built a miniature of the real ablation — a shared trunk with axis / origin / point heads, finite noisy data, held-out eval — and added a trajectory head (arm-D-style), a detached-trunk trajectory head, and an analytic decode (H1-style). Result: null across the board (baseline 34.9°, aux head 37.0°, analytic 35.2° — nothing helps). Repeated later in an underfitting regime (nonlinear teacher, capacity-limited student): null again (41.7 / 42.5 / 44.6°).

Dead: "generic multi-task transfer explains it." Redundant auxiliary supervision on a plain MLP trunk reproduces nothing. The real effect needs something the toy lacks — so only a real-scale experiment could decide.

3The discriminating experiment: the analytic screw decode

The clean way to separate H1 from H2: give the model the trajectory loss without the trajectory head. We deleted the head entirely (arm B's exact config) and instead decoded the trajectory deterministically and differentiably from the predicted articulation parameters — an exact mirror of the dataset writer (ray for trans, right-hand arc for rot), proven exact by tests that AST-extract the writer's own code. The standard normalized trajectory loss is applied to that decode. Zero new parameters.

Experiment 20260825_sf3d_analytic_decode. If this arm matches arm D, the gain is the loss's geometry (H1) — the head was never necessary for articulation. If it stays at arm B, the gain rode on the head's feature shaping (H2).

4The result: 75% of the gain, zero parameters

arm B — no trajectory analytic decode (0 params) arm D — trajectory head
Articulation metrics, standard 5,088-sample test. The zero-parameter decode lands almost on top of the full trajectory head: MA recovers 6.1 of the 7.8-point gap, matched axis 4.3° of 6.2°, and most of the flip-rate improvement — without any gradient ever touching the shared features through a trajectory pathway.

And the counter-panel, which is just as important — the masks invert:

Perception metrics. The decode adds articulation-gradient pressure on pooled features without the dense spatial supervision a real head provides — and the trunk pays. The mask gains that trajectory-bearing arms enjoy are the head's doing, not the loss's.
Verdict — the split, measured. The articulation transfer is ~75% loss geometry (H1): expressing axis ∧ origin ∧ point as one curve is a denser, jointly-constrained, GT-anchored objective, and it works even when its gradients bypass the shared features entirely. The trajectory head's own contribution (H2) is the mask/trunk side — which the decode conspicuously fails to reproduce. Both hypotheses were right, about different metrics. A redundant label helps because the same information in a better-conditioned parameterization is a different optimization problem.

5Closing the loop: the toy's final word, and corroboration

With the scale verdict in hand we returned to the toy for a demonstration — and it refused a third time (underfitting regime: baseline 41.7°, aux head 42.5°, analytic 44.6°). That's now a finding, not a failure: the effect is irreducible to a generic low-dimensional mechanism. It requires the real perception system — structured visual features, the indirect heatmap / depth-lift head pathways, the optimization regime at scale. The honest artifact is the pair: at-scale attribution (this study) + small-scale irreducibility (viz/20260825_toy_traj_mechanism/, 4 panels).

Two independent results from the same week corroborate the verdict:

The fdiff losses' success makes sense now. First-difference losses are trajectory-side supervision — more loss geometry on the same quantities — and they hold the MA record (29.9). The sign information genuinely lives in the trajectory's time ordering: removing the trajectory nearly doubled rot flips in the ablation (14.8 → 21.8).
The dir-term saga fits. An explicit sign-consistency term failed on the DCT head (its low-frequency basis funnels the term's trajectory drag into exactly the net-direction mode) but achieved its design goal on the plain head — rot flips 15.2 → 11.24, the best ever recorded. Representation and loss geometry interact; neither story is complete alone.

6What this buys us

1. A free articulation loss. The analytic decode costs nothing at inference, adds no parameters, and delivers most of the trajectory benefit straight into the articulation heads — usable even in models with no trajectory head at all.

2. A design principle. When two heads' quantities are physically coupled, supervise the coupled object (the curve), not only the marginals — but route dense spatial supervision through a real head if you also want the trunk to benefit.

The gen-22 candidate, with every part attributed: trajectory head (for masks) + analytic decode (for articulation) + fdiff losses (direction/MA) + plain-head or trajectory-detached dir term (sign) — L_pp measured, not assumed (its value flipped sign between head families).
