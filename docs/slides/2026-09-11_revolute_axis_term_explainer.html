Why the revolute trajectory loss cannot carry the axis sign — and what can

The prismatic trajectory loss turned out to be the 1 − cos axis loss. This page explains why the revolute one does not, what it does instead, the single formulation that would, and why the separate axis loss is the principled partner rather than an afterthought. Source: knowledge/2026-09-11_revolute_loss_with_axis_term.md (every formula verified numerically, script tools/revolute_loss_check.py).

1. The setup: what a revolute trajectory is made of

The model predicts an axis direction n, a hinge origin o, and the interaction point p₀. Ground truth is n*, o*, p₀*. The point's path is a circular arc, and everything about that arc is captured by two vectors:

  • the lever \(\mathbf r = (I - \mathbf n\mathbf n^{\top})(\mathbf p_0 - \mathbf o)\) — from the axis line to the point, perpendicular to the axis. Its length is the radius.
  • the tangent \(\mathbf t = \mathbf n \times \mathbf r\) — the direction the point starts moving. This is the only place the axis sign enters: flip \(\mathbf n\) and \(\mathbf t\) flips.
\[\mathbf x(\theta) = \mathbf o + \cos\theta\,\mathbf r + \sin\theta\,\mathbf t, \qquad \theta \in [0, \Theta]\]

The loss compares this arc with the GT arc at matched angles θ, integrates over the sweep Θ (π/2 in the writer), and divides by the same integral on the GT arc so a collapsed prediction scores 1.

2. Why prismatic gives 1 − cos for free

A prismatic trajectory is a straight segment of fixed length L along the predicted direction d̂. The squared distance to the GT segment, integrated and normalised, is

\[\frac{\int_0^1 \lVert sL\hat{\mathbf d} - sL\hat{\mathbf d}^*\rVert^2\,ds}{\int_0^1 \lVert sL\hat{\mathbf d}^*\rVert^2\,ds} \;=\; \lVert \hat{\mathbf d} - \hat{\mathbf d}^*\rVert^2 \;=\; 2\bigl(1 - \cos\angle(\hat{\mathbf d}, \hat{\mathbf d}^*)\bigr)\]

The length cancels under the normalisation, nothing else is left, and what remains is exactly the sign-aware axis loss: scale-free, and with its steepest gradient at the flipped direction. Nothing was designed here; it is the only thing a segment knows about.

3. The master formula for every revolute point-arc loss

Expanding the revolute quadratic in the frame of the two arcs gives one form that covers the position term, the derivative term, any sweep and any normaliser. With \(k = \mathbf n\cdot\mathbf n^*\) (the axis alignment), \(\lambda = \lVert\mathbf r\rVert / \lVert\mathbf r^*\rVert\) (predicted over GT radius) and two in-plane phases \(\psi\) and \(\chi\):

\[\mathcal L \;=\; \underbrace{\mathrm{radius}(\lambda)}_{\text{radius error}} \;+\; \underbrace{\lambda^{p}\,(1-k)\,\bigl[1 + \rho_\Theta \cos(\chi - \chi_\Theta)\bigr]}_{\text{axis alignment}} \;+\; \underbrace{\lambda^{p}\,(1+k)\,(1-\cos\psi)}_{\text{lever phase}}\]

Three pieces: a radius error, an axis-alignment term that is a \(1-\cos\) but multiplied by two factors, and a lever-phase term (how far the lever is rotated in-plane).

So the 1 − cos is there. The problem is the two multipliers in front of it:

Mechanism 1 — homogeneity (\(p = 1\)): the axis gradient shrinks with the radius

The numerator is quadratic in the predicted lever and the denominator uses only the GT lever, so every axis-carrying term is multiplied by \(\lambda\). At a predicted radius of 1 cm on a 1 m door the axis gradient is 1 % of normal; at zero lever the loss is exactly 1 for every axis — a plateau where the axis is invisible. A scale-free term like \(1-k\) has degree 0 in the lever and simply cannot arise from a degree-2 numerator over a GT-only normaliser.

Mechanism 2 — phase leak (\(\rho_\Theta > 0\)): the flipped axis can be bought back

The coefficient of \((1-k)\) is \(1 + \rho_\Theta\cos(\chi - \chi_\Theta)\), and the phase \(\chi\) is set by where the origin and point are placed. So the other heads can shrink the flip penalty to \((1-\rho_\Theta)\). For the position term \(\rho_\Theta \ge 0.5\) at every sweep, because its basis \(\{\cos\theta - 1,\ \sin\theta\}\) is never isotropic. For the derivative term \(\rho_\Theta = \lvert\sin\Theta\rvert/\Theta\), which vanishes at \(\Theta = \pi\) and \(2\pi\).

4. The one clean instance

A clean, scale-free, uncompensable \((1-k)\) appears exactly when \(p = 0\) (normalise by the predicted lever too, or compare unit tangents) and \(\rho_\Theta = 0\) (derivative term at \(\Theta = \pi\) or \(2\pi\)). The simplest form is the derivative loss at \(\Theta = \pi\) on unit-normalised levers:

\[\mathcal L_{\text{rot\_der\_unit}} \;=\; \tfrac12\bigl(\lVert\hat{\mathbf r} - \hat{\mathbf r}^*\rVert^2 + \lVert\hat{\mathbf t} - \hat{\mathbf t}^*\rVert^2\bigr) \;=\; (1-k) + (1+k)(1-\cos\psi)\]

Two lines in closed_form_screw_loss: normalise \(\mathbf r\) and \(\mathbf t\) (with a floor on \(\lVert\mathbf r\rVert\)) and set the sweep to \(\pi\). The flipped axis costs exactly 2 whatever the origin and point do; the axis gradient is the same at any radius.

What it gives up, and why it is still not the axis loss:

  • No radius. Unit levers discard \(\lVert\mathbf r\rVert\); the existing position term at a small weight (0.1–0.2) has to carry radius and origin.
  • A singularity when the predicted axis is parallel to \(\mathbf p_0 - \mathbf o\) (the projected lever vanishes); needs the floor.
  • Half the escape routes. At the flipped axis the curvature in the two axis directions is (−1, +1): tilting the axis one way lowers the loss, tilting it the other way raises it. The direct axis loss has (−1, −1): every tilt helps. A point arc only sees the axis through the tangent direction, whose phase is a nuisance the origin controls, so this is the best any point-arc functional can do.

5. The rigid-body picture: why the two joint types differ

A joint moves the whole part, not just one point. Write the motion as a rigid transform: a rotation and a translation.

  • Prismatic: rotation \(= I\), translation \(= \theta L\,\hat{\mathbf d}\). The whole motion is the direction, and any point's path shows it. That is why the segment loss collapsed to \(1-\cos\).
  • Revolute: rotation \(= R(\theta\mathbf n)\), translation \(= (I - R(\theta\mathbf n))\,\mathbf o\). The direction lives in the rotation part. A single point's arc is the translation part evaluated at that point — it sees the axis only indirectly, through the lever.

Now take the trajectory loss of the orientation, the derivative (\(H^1\)) version: the body angular velocity of \(R(\theta\mathbf n)\) is the constant vector \(\mathbf n\), so

\[\frac{1}{2\Theta}\int_0^{\Theta} \bigl\lVert R^{\top}\dot R - R^{*\top}\dot R^{*}\bigr\rVert_F^2\,d\theta \;=\; \lVert\mathbf n - \mathbf n^*\rVert^2 \;=\; 2\,(1-k)\]

which is the same expression the prismatic rows produce. The direct axis loss is the revolute twin of the prismatic trajectory loss. It was in the recipe from the start for empirical reasons; this is the reason it belongs there.

Side result: the position (\(L^2\)) loss on the rotation matrices, \(\int \lVert R(\theta\mathbf n) - R(\theta\mathbf n^*)\rVert_F^2\,d\theta = 8(\Theta - \sin\Theta)(1-k) - 2A(1-k)^2\), is a concave reshaping of \(1-\cos\) that becomes non-monotone for sweeps above about 2.1 rad (at \(\Theta = \pi\) the flipped axis is a local minimum, because a reversed full turn visits the same orientations). Do not use it at large sweeps.

6. What this means for the runs

  1. Keep the axis loss on. It is not an extra; it is the missing half of the revolute trajectory loss, with the only curvature shape (−1, −1) that closes every escape from a flipped axis. Every record recipe already has it at 0.5.
  2. The planned first run stands: the L2 closed form plus the standard axis loss on SF3D — the untested corner of the August grid — with the decoder and projection loss on the hand-video sources. Adding the derivative (H1) term later is the same functional one derivative up.
  3. If a single trajectory term should carry the sign, the composition is: unit-lever \(H^1\) at \(\Theta = \pi\) (shape and sign) + the current position term at 0.1–0.2 (radius and origin) + the axis loss at 0.5. The experiment that tests the theory is unit-lever \(H^1\) with no axis loss against the no-axis arm's 15.9 % flips.
  4. Two things not to do: the \(SO(3)\) position loss at sweeps \(\ge 2.1\) rad, and a geometric-mean normaliser \(1/(\lVert\mathbf r\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf r^*\rVert)\) without a lever floor — its origin gradient grows like \(1/\lambda^2\) at small radius.
