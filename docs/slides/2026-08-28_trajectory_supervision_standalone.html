What trajectory supervision buys — and why

Our model looks at one RGB-D image of an articulated object (a cabinet, a laptop, a trash can) plus a text instruction, and predicts how the part moves. We supervise it two ways. This page reports a controlled study of what each supervision path contributes — with a surprising answer about why one of them works.

SegAffordance · four training runs, identical everything except supervision · each evaluated on the same 5,088-image held-out test set

1Setup: two ways of describing the same motion

For every training image the dataset provides two descriptions of the part's motion:

Articulation parameters (compact)
Motion type (rotation vs sliding), the 3D axis direction, the hinge origin point, and the 3D interaction point on the part — about nine numbers. The model has a small prediction head for each.
Trajectory (verbose)
A curve of 20 3D points tracing the path the part sweeps when the motion is executed — a straight ray for sliding parts, an arc around the hinge for rotating ones. The model has a separate head that predicts these 60 numbers, trained with a point-matching loss plus first-difference losses (they compare consecutive point-to-point steps of the predicted and true curves, supervising per-segment direction and length).

The catch that motivated this study: the trajectory labels are computed from the articulation labels. Given axis, origin, type, and the part's location, a fixed geometric formula generates the curve — the trajectory contains zero information the compact labels don't already carry. Naively, adding it to training should change nothing. It changes a lot.

2The experiment: four models, one variable

■ Articulation-only
Trajectory head deleted, trajectory losses gone. Only the compact labels teach the model.
■ Trajectory-only
The opposite: articulation heads deleted; only the curve teaches the model.
■ Both paths (joint)
Everything on — the normal recipe. (No auxiliary consistency losses in any arm, so the paths interact only through the shared image features.)
■ The formula arm — trajectory loss without a trajectory network
The diagnostic. The trajectory head is deleted, but during training we take the model's own predicted axis/origin/point, run them through the same geometric formula that generated the labels (differentiably), and apply the trajectory losses to that computed curve. The model gains zero parameters — it never "learns" trajectories — yet its articulation heads now feel the trajectory losses directly. If this arm matches the joint model, the benefit of trajectory supervision was never the extra network; it was the loss.
The formula arm. A prediction is only rewarded when axis, origin, and point are jointly right — that's what placing the curve requires — and a wrong axis direction sweeps the curve the wrong way, making sign errors expensive. Same information as the compact losses, very different shape.

Metrics used below: MA — fraction of test samples where the part is correctly detected and its predicted axis is within tolerance (the headline articulation score, higher better). Matched axis error — mean angular error of the axis on correctly-detected samples (lower better). Flip rate — how often the predicted rotation axis points the wrong way (backwards door swing). mIoU — part-mask segmentation quality.

3Finding 1: training both paths together beats either alone

articulation-only trajectory-onlyboth
Left: the articulation heads improve enormously when the curve is co-trained — despite the curve carrying no new information. Right: the trajectory head also improves when articulation is co-trained, by a much smaller margin. The coupling is strongly asymmetric: trajectory teaches articulation ~5× more than the reverse.

4Finding 2: it was never the network — ~90% of the gain is the loss itself

articulation-only formula arm (0 extra params)both (full head)
The formula arm — trajectory losses applied to a curve computed from the model's own articulation predictions, zero added parameters — recovers ~89% of the joint model's articulation gain, beats it on wrong-way axes and hinge-origin error, and concedes only some axis sharpness. The trajectory network is not where the value lives.
Why a redundant label helps. The curve re-expresses the same nine numbers as sixty coupled ones: to place it correctly, axis ∧ origin ∧ point must be right together, a wrong axis direction sweeps it backwards (large, obvious error), and every point contributes gradient. Same information, better-shaped objective. The practical corollary is free performance: any model — even one with no trajectory head — can get most of this benefit by decoding its articulation predictions through the label formula and applying the trajectory losses there.

5Finding 3: the cost side — segmentation pays

Every form of trajectory pressure costs some segmentation quality, and the full joint model pays the most. The mechanism: the trajectory losses back-propagate through features pooled from the part region, and their gradients compete with what the mask decoder wants from those same pixels.
So there is no free lunch, only a favorable trade: trajectory supervision converts a little segmentation quality (−0.02 mIoU) into a lot of articulation quality (+7 MA, axis error down a third, wrong-way axes down 40%). And the formula arm buys most of that at the smallest mask cost.

ΣTakeaways

1. Supervising a target that is mathematically derived from labels you already use can still improve a model dramatically — information content and training value are different things.

2. The value lives in the loss geometry: dense, jointly-constrained, direction-aware error signals. It can be captured with zero extra parameters by decoding predictions through the label-generating formula.

3. The coupling is asymmetric: motion curves teach articulation parameters far more than articulation parameters teach curves.

4. The price is a small segmentation dip — a trade to make deliberately, not an accident to discover later.
